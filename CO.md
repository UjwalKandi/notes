### TUESDAY PHOTOGRAPHY DETAIL

**5:31 AM — Many Parks Curve, Trail Ridge Road**

Entry: $35/vehicle or Annual Pass. Gate opens at 5:00 AM — drive straight in.

Many Parks Curve sits at ~9,600 feet on the eastern slope of Trail Ridge Road. From here, multiple glacially-carved mountain parks (wide alpine meadows) spread across the valley below, each bordered by forested ridgelines receding into the distance. At sunrise the closest ridges are in warm light while the further ones remain cool blue — this layered tonal depth is what makes the shot.

- **70–200mm** — compress the ridgelines into stacked layers. The key composition is 4–5 ridges receding into the frame at different luminosity values
- **16–24mm** — the full curve of the road with the valley panorama. Include foreground tundra for depth
- Wind is minimal before 6 AM. Shoot fast — Trail Ridge catches wind early

**Trail Ridge Road progression (6:30–9:00 AM):**

*Forest Canyon Overlook* — A 2,500-foot drop into a U-shaped glacially carved canyon directly below. The canyon runs east-west so morning light creates strong shadow in the valley floor. Telephoto for isolating the canyon floor river. Wide for the full canyon width with tundra foreground.

*Alpine Visitor Center area* — At 11,796 feet, you are above treeline. In early June, the tundra has alpine wildflowers just opening and remnant snow patches. Low angle macro shots of tiny yellow and white blooms against snow — a distinctly Colorado frame that requires no hiking, just crouching on the tundra boardwalk.

*Milner Pass (11,183 ft)* — The Continental Divide marker sits in a small saddle. Two sides of a continent drain away from this point — the Poudre River east, the Colorado River west. Simple graphic composition: the sign against blue sky.

**11:30 AM — Twin Lakes**
Two glacial lakes at the base of the Collegiate Peaks. In calm conditions the reflection of Mount Elbert (14,440 ft, Colorado's highest) and Mount Massive (14,421 ft) in the still water is strong. Midday light is not ideal but the reflection quality depends on wind, not light angle. Worth 45 minutes.

**12:45 PM — Independence Pass (12,095 ft)**
One of the highest paved roads in North America. At the summit: panoramic views of the Sawatch Range with the road switchbacking down both sides. The tarmac ribbon cutting through the alpine terrain is the primary composition — shoot the road itself, not just the peaks. Afternoon is actually decent light here because the road faces multiple directions.

**5:25 PM – 8:30 PM — Maroon Bells Evening Scout**

The twin 14,000-foot Maroon Bells are northwest-facing. They catch warm afternoon and evening light. In early June, snow still clings to the upper slopes. Your objective tonight is dual — shoot the evening light on the peaks, AND scout your exact sunrise position at Maroon Lake for tomorrow morning.

Walk to the lakeshore and find:
- The northeast end of the lake for the widest bell-peaks reflection with aspen framing on both sides
- The widest reflection angle where both peaks appear in the water simultaneously
- The position that minimizes other photographers in your frame at 5:33 AM

Golden hour here (7:45–8:20 PM): The peaks go from orange to deep amber as the light drops. This is a strong portfolio session in its own right even before the sunrise shoot.

**Sleep logistics:**
Drive back ~3 miles down Maroon Creek Road toward Aspen. Around Mile 7–8, Forest Service dispersed pullouts are common and photographers use them specifically for early Maroon Bells access. You are 7 minutes from the lake. The alternative is sleeping in the main Maroon Lake parking lot itself — photographers do this regularly, and before 8 AM you have self-drive access without the shuttle.

### WEDNESDAY PHOTOGRAPHY DETAIL

**5:33 AM — Maroon Bells Lake Reflection**
*Timed entry — self-drive before 8 AM requires no reservation. After 8 AM shuttle system activates.*

You slept 7 minutes away. You walk to your pre-scouted position in the dark. This is the exact advantage of car sleeping on this trip — no pre-dawn 45-minute drive from Aspen, no risk of being locked out because you arrived at 8:02 AM.

The Maroon Bells are two 14,163 ft and 14,156 ft peaks reflected in Maroon Lake. In June, snow remains on the upper slopes and the surrounding aspens are in full green leaf. This combination — snow-capped peaks, green aspen shoreline, still water reflection — is the most photographed image in Colorado.

**The reflection window:**
The lake surface is stillest from 4:45–6:30 AM before wind develops. The peaks themselves are still in shadow at 5:33 AM — first light hits the upper spires, and the reflection catches this warm spot above a cool blue lower canyon. Shoot this transition continuously.

- **16–24mm** — full reflection, both peaks, aspen framing on both sides of the lake
- **50mm** — cleaner sky-to-reflection ratio, less distortion
- **70–200mm** — peak isolation with snow detail in the upper couloirs
- Shoot horizontal and vertical — horizontal for the full panorama, vertical for the peak-to-reflection symmetry

**8:00 AM — CO-82 Aspen Valley Road**
Heading east from Aspen, CO-82 runs through a spectacular mountain corridor — Castle Peak and the Elk Mountains to the south, the road lined with cottonwoods and aspens in morning light. Morning sidelight on the valley road creates cinematic frames. Drive slowly and pull over at any composition that reads. 45 minutes here between Aspen and Basalt produces road photography that is uniquely Colorado.

**Curecanti NRA / Blue Mesa Reservoir — 10:00 AM**
Zero detour — US-50 runs directly along the reservoir. Pull at any of the overlooks. Colorado's largest reservoir with deep blue water against pale canyon walls. The color contrast is strongest in flat midday light, counterintuitively — the cool blue reads more saturated when there's no warm shadow to compete with.

**11:30 AM — Black Canyon of the Gunnison**
Entry: $35 or Annual Pass.

The canyon drops 2,722 feet to the Gunnison River — in places the canyon is only 40 feet wide at river level. The darkness of the rock (Precambrian gneiss and schist, 1.8 billion years old) against the pale sky gives it a visual weight unlike any other canyon in America.

*Gunnison Point* — Primary overlook. The main canyon view. Wide angle for the full drop, telephoto to isolate the canyon layers. In midday the canyon floor is in full shadow while the rim is in flat light — still a strong exposure because of the extreme luminance difference.

*Painted Wall* — Colorado's tallest cliff face at 2,250 feet. The name comes from white and pink pegmatite veins streaking through the dark rock face — they look like paint brushstrokes. Telephoto to bring in the vein detail. The wall is on the opposite side of the canyon from the south rim — you are photographing across the void.

*Dragon Point, Sunset View, High Point* — East Rim overlooks accessible by the rim road. Less visited than Gunnison Point. Different canyon angles, different rock exposures.

**6:00–8:30 PM — Chasm View**
The best canyon sunset position on the South Rim. The canyon runs roughly east-west — at sunset, the light comes from the west and rakes across the southern canyon wall face. The canyon shadows deepen from dark brown to near-black while the rim rocks above hold warm orange light. The luminance contrast at peak golden hour (7:45–8:20 PM) is extreme — the lit rim against the black canyon void is the shot.

After sunset (8:20–8:30 PM): The canyon fills with deep blue while the sky above holds a last warm gradient. Shoot the post-sunset window for 10 minutes before light fails completely.

### THURSDAY PHOTOGRAPHY DETAIL

**5:35 AM — Ouray Mountain Bowl Sunrise**
Ouray sits in a natural amphitheater at 7,792 feet, surrounded on three sides by 13,000–14,000-foot walls. The Amphitheater Campground above the south end of town, or the overlook on the Perimeter Trail above town, gives you the full bowl view. At sunrise, the upper peaks catch warm alpenglow while the town below remains in cool shadow. The contrast of the illuminated peaks against the dark valley communicates the bowl geography perfectly.

**7:15 AM — Blue Lakes Hike**

The trailhead sits off Dallas Creek Road (dirt, passable in any standard vehicle). The trail gains 1,800 feet over 2.75 miles to reach the lower lake, then another 600 feet to the upper lakes. Budget 3.5–4 hours round trip. In mid-June, the upper lakes at ~12,000 feet may have snow and ice on their edges with turquoise glacier-fed water beneath. Mount Sneffels (14,158 ft) and the surrounding peaks of the Sneffels Wilderness form the backdrop.

**Key shot at Blue Lakes:** The turquoise color of the water against remnant snowpack and dark rock is completely unlike any other water shot on the trip. Wide angle from the shoreline — include foreground rock and snow at the water's edge for depth. Telephoto to compress Mount Sneffels against the lake surface.

**Altitude note:** You are at 12,000+ feet after flying from Austin the day before yesterday. Hydrate aggressively. If any headache or nausea develops on the way up, turn back. This is the highest sustained elevation of the trip.

**Bear Creek Falls**
3 miles south of Ouray on US-550 — a roadside pullout, 10-minute stop. In June at peak snowmelt the falls pour off the canyon wall directly beside the highway. Shoot the falls with the canyon walls and highway guard rail as foreground context.

**Red Mountain Pass — Midday and Golden Hour**

The iron oxide (rust) in the surrounding rock gives the three Red Mountains their vivid rust-orange and deep red color. This is not brown rock or grey rock — it's genuinely red, and in good light it reads as almost unnaturally saturated.

The pass sits at 11,018 feet on US-550 between Ouray (north) and Silverton (south). You pass through it twice today — first going south to Silverton in the late morning, then returning north for the golden hour session.

**Midday pass (11:50 AM):** Scout your golden hour position. Identify the pullouts with the clearest southwest-facing views toward the red peaks. The road curving away toward Silverton with the rust peaks behind is the primary composition.

**Golden hour (5:00–8:30 PM):** The 3.5-hour window here is the longest single golden hour session of the trip. The iron-oxide peaks shift through an extraordinary color sequence from warm orange at 7:45 PM through deep crimson-red to a final purple-red before dark. At peak (8:00–8:15 PM), the peaks are a color intensity that most photographers have never seen on a mountain.

- Leading line: the highway curving south toward Silverton with peak behind
- The high-contrast composition: rust peaks against saturated blue sky before clouds roll in
- Detail: the oxidized rock texture in the cliff faces with telephoto
- Environmental: the mining ruins visible along the road below the pass in context with the mountains

**Silverton (12:40–4:30 PM)**
A preserved 1880s silver mining town at 9,318 feet. The entire downtown is on the National Register of Historic Places. Blair Street (the historic red-light district) has intact Victorian-era storefronts. Greene Street is the main commercial strip. Shoot the old town architecture in afternoon sidelight — the weathered wood and painted metal signs read well at 2–4 PM.

The narrow-gauge Durango & Silverton Railroad terminates here. If a steam train is in the station, it's a strong composition.

**Driving Thursday night:**
The route from Red Mountain Pass to Buena Vista via Ridgway, Montrose, Gunnison, and Monarch Pass is ~150 miles and 3 hours. Leaving at 8:30 PM puts you in Buena Vista around 11:30 PM. This is a real drive — mountain roads in the dark after a full day. Stock up on coffee in Gunnison (~10:00 PM on the route) before Monarch Pass.

### FRIDAY PHOTOGRAPHY DETAIL

**5:32 AM — Collegiate Peaks, Buena Vista Corridor**

You slept a few hundred yards from the viewpoints on CR-306 or US-24. Zero driving to the golden hour.

The Collegiate Peaks are a cluster of fourteen 14,000-foot summits named after universities — Harvard (14,420 ft), Yale (14,196 ft), Columbia (14,073 ft), Oxford (14,160 ft), Princeton (14,197 ft). From the valley floor on US-24 and CR-306, they rise as a continuous wall to the west. The Arkansas River runs in the valley below — in June at peak snowmelt, the river is high and fast.

In early morning alpenglow (5:00–5:32 AM) the upper peaks glow pink-red while the valley is in pre-dawn blue. At sunrise the alpenglow transitions to warm amber. The Arkansas River valley with the peak wall behind it gives you layers — river, meadow, foothills, peaks.

- CR-306 (Cottonwood Lake Road) offers pullouts with clear westward sightlines
- US-24 north of Buena Vista has several roadside pullouts with the peak chain visible
- Wide angle for the full mountain wall and valley

**9:30 AM — Garden of the Gods**
Entry: Free.

The red Pikes Peak Sandstone formations here face east — which makes this a morning photography location, not an afternoon one. You arrive at 9:30 AM, still in good directional light from the east.

*Siamese Twins* — The defining shot: two rock formations create a natural arch that frames Pikes Peak (14,115 ft) perfectly in the gap between them. Use a telephoto (200mm+) from the marked viewpoint to bring the mountain forward through the arch. This single frame summarizes the entire Colorado Springs visit.

*Cathedral Spires* — Three vertical formations shooting upward. Wide angle from below exaggerates height dramatically.

*Balanced Rock* — A 700-ton sandstone boulder on a narrow base. Morning light creates strong shadow definition on the balanced form. Also excellent at blue hour (post-sunset) if you were staying for evening photography.

*Kissing Camels* — The two arched formations visible from the main parking area. Good foreground for a composition including Pikes Peak behind.

### SATURDAY PHOTOGRAPHY DETAIL

**5:38 AM — Great Sand Dunes Sunrise**
Entry: $35 or Annual Pass.

You are already parked in the lot. The walk from the parking area to the first significant dune ridge is about 10 minutes across flat sandy terrain. Be on a ridge by 5:15 AM to watch the pre-dawn sequence build.

**The pre-dawn window (5:00–5:38 AM):**
The Sangre de Cristo Mountains sit directly east of the dunes — as the sky brightens, the mountain peaks behind you catch alpenglow while the dune crests in front of you remain in deep blue shadow. The contrast between warm illuminated mountain peaks and dark cold dunes is a compelling pre-sunrise frame.

**Sunrise (5:38 AM):**
First direct light arrives from the east and rakes across the dune surface at an extreme low angle — the shadow-to-light transition on a single dune crest is almost architectural. Every wind ripple in the sand throws a shadow. The dune ridges become a series of sharp luminance contrasts that read as pure graphic form.

The first 30 minutes of light (5:38–6:10 AM) are the prime window. After 6:10 AM the sun rises higher and the raking angle softens.

**What to shoot:**
- **Dune ridge graphic** — Camera low to the sand (3–6 inches), wide angle, shooting along a dune ridge into the sun. The ridge line leads across the frame with shadow on one side and light on the other. This is the minimalist dune shot.
- **Scale shot** — Position yourself on a distant ridge before sunrise. From below, shoot your silhouette on the 750-foot dune crest against the brightening sky. Self-timer or trigger.
- **Texture close-up** — Macro or close focus on wind ripple patterns in the sand surface in raking first light. The patterns are perfect geometric forms.
- **Medano Creek** — If the seasonal stream is still flowing along the dune base in mid-June, it creates a rare triple landscape in one frame: turquoise flowing water in the foreground, enormous sand dunes, and snow-capped Sangre de Cristo peaks behind. Check current flow status on the park website before arriving.
- **Sand vs. mountains** — Wide angle looking east from the base of the dunes. The flat sandy desert in the foreground, dune walls in the middle, and the 14,000-foot mountains rising directly behind — three completely different landscapes stacked in one composition.

**9:12 AM — Zapata Falls**
9 miles south on CO-150, then a short drive up a dirt road to the trailhead. 0.8-mile flat walk into a narrow granite slot canyon. The falls drop 40 feet through the slot in a compressed rush of white water. In June at peak snowmelt, the volume is full force. Bring a rain cover — the spray radius is significant. Shoot at 1/30–1/60 second for flowing water motion blur. The canyon walls are only 10–15 feet apart — wide angle is the only option.
